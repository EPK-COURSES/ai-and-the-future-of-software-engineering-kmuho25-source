Candida albicans synergizes with Fusobacterium nucleatum in colorectal cancer progression via the Flo9-RadD interaction

Summary

Fusobacterium nucleatum tumor infiltration is a hallmark of colorectal cancer (CRC) progression. However, whether and how other microbes influence F. nucleatum in CRC remains unclear. Here, we discover that the commensal fungus Candida albicans synergizes with F. nucleatum to promote CRC pathogenesis and contributes to poor patient outcomes. Co-inoculation of C. albicans and F. nucleatum accelerates CRC progression compared to either microbe alone in animal models. C. albicans strains isolated from CRC patients exhibiting enhanced hyphal morphogenesis and elevated FLO9 expression promote F. nucleatum-mediated tumor growth. Mechanistically, through the Flo9-RadD interaction, C. albicans facilitates F. nucleatum localization to the colonic mucosa and promotes F. nucleatum-driven malignant transformation. Translationally, we demonstrate that L-arginine disrupts the interaction between C. albicans and F. nucleatum, reducing their synergistic pro-tumor activity in CRC models in vivo. These findings highlight fungal-bacterial interaction as a critical etiologic mechanism in CRC and a potential therapeutic target.

Introduction

The gut microbiota, which includes bacteria, viruses, and fungi, is fundamentally important to the various functions of the gastrointestinal tract, including nutrient absorption, metabolism, immune defense, and intestinal epithelial differentiation. Microbial dysbiosis in the large intestine causes a spectrum of physiological disorders, and it has particularly been linked to cancer and cancer outcomes.1,2 In particular, the progression of colorectal cancer (CRC), one of the most common cancers worldwide, is profoundly influenced by gut microbiota.3
Recent studies have pinpointed the involvement of bacterial species, such as Fusobacterium nucleatum, in modulating the pathogenesis of colorectal tumors.4 By binding to cell-surface receptors via adhesins, F. nucleatum can enter tumor or immune cells, where they play a regulatory role in tumor-promoting inflammation and malignant progression.5,6 Although F. nucleatum potentiates intestinal tumorigenesis in the specific pathogen-free (SPF) ApcMin/+ mouse model,7 in a recent study using germ-free (GF) ApcMin/+ mouse models, F. nucleatum alone failed to enhance tumorigenesis,8 suggesting that other microbial species might play a role in facilitating its tumor-promoting effects. Based on the findings of metagenomics profiling, there has been some speculation about how other types of microbe, potentially including fungal species from the microbiota, directly influence or synergize with bacterial species to impact the progression of CRC,9,10,11 suggesting that multiple species may be involved in the carcinogenesis. However, there have been few mechanistic studies on this issue.
Fungal microorganisms (collectively described as the mycobiota) constitute a small (0.1%–1%) proportion of the human gut microbiota.12,13 Candida albicans is the most prevalent fungal species with immunomodulatory effects.14,15,16 C. albicans is a dimorphic fungus that exists in yeast or hyphal forms in the mammalian gut.17,18,19 Various species of microbes may compete or work in synergy to limit or promote their colonization of the gut.20,21 However, it remains unclear how specific fungal species exert their effects on the host to affect cancer development. In this study, we explored the role of fungal species in human CRC in four cohorts and found that co-colonization of F. nucleatum and C. albicans was predictive of poor clinical outcomes. Furthermore, the clinical isolates of C. albicans displayed differing capabilities of extensive hyphal growth and high expression of FLO9 being associated with poor CRC prognosis. We also demonstrated that C. albicans facilitates F. nucleatum localization to colonic mucosa via Flo9 (a fungal factor) and RadD (a bacterial factor) as mediators of direct physical interaction between these two microbes, a process essential for F. nucleatum’s oncogenic effect, and disrupting this protein-protein interaction with L-arginine could reduce their synergistic effect in CRC models in vivo.

    • A cross-kingdom alliance driving colorectal cancer

      2026, Cancer Cell
      In this issue of Cancer Cell, Li et al. show that Candida albicans and Fusobacterium nucleatum form a cross-kingdom interaction through Flo9-RadD binding that accelerates colorectal cancer (CRC) progression. Disrupting this interaction with L-arginine reduces tumor burden, highlighting targeting microbial cooperation as a potential therapeutic strategy for CRC.

    • Tumor-associated bacterial effectors: novel frontiers in tumor biology and immunity

      2026, Science Bulletin
      The complex and dynamic bacterial community symbiotically interacts with the host in many body niches and participates in various physiological and pathological states, including cancer. Advances in biotechnology have shifted the research focus from merely profiling bacterial abundance to identifying the functionally relevant bacteria-derived effectors that modulate tumor biology and immune responses. Here, we introduce a conceptual classification framework designating these effectors as tumor-associated bacterial effectors (TABEs), aiming to consolidate and direct ongoing investigations. Under this proposed taxonomy, TABEs can be classified into six distinct categories: virulence factors, microorganism-associated molecular patterns (MAMPs), bacterial metabolites, biotransformative enzymes, cross-reactive antigens, and noncoding RNAs (ncRNAs). We comprehensively summarize their functional mechanisms and multifaceted roles in tumor progression and immunomodulation. Based on these insights, we propose multidimensional potential therapeutic strategies targeting TABEs. Furthermore, we discuss current limitations that warrant further investigations and emerging trends that may shape future research directions in this field. Collectively, in-depth investigations of TABEs may help elucidate the complex human–bacteria crosstalk and expand the paradigm of tumor biology and immunity.
